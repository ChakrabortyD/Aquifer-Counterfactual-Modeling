What the rules bought

Pumping cutbacks have been mandated in the Edwards Aquifer since 2006. Counterfactual AI answers the question a monitoring record never can — what would the springs and the water table have done if they had not?

Sharma, Başağaoğlu, Yoosefdoost, Wootten, Chakraborty-Reddy, Bertetti, Mirchi & Chakraborty. Every figure on this page is computed from the study’s published Code Ocean capsule — no model was re-fit.

The evidence gap

Almost every aquifer has protections. Almost none has proof.

A survey of 710 aquifers worldwide, drawn from 2,224 published articles and reports, found mitigation measures proposed or established at 642 of them — 90%. What it did not find was evidence that those measures work.

Those 710 aquifers host 1,058 endangered and 651 threatened species. 429 of them are karst systems — fast-draining limestone, like the Edwards, where a drought reaches the water table in weeks rather than decades.

Only about 27% of published studies on subterranean conservation consider groundwater habitats at all, and they rarely test whether an intervention changed anything. Many programmes have been monitoring exercises, during which listed species went extinct.

The Edwards Aquifer is the rare case that can settle the question: it has mandated cutbacks, a long instrumental record through the drought of record in the 1950s, and endemic species whose survival depends on the answer.

How a counterfactual is built

Train a model on a world without rules, then let it keep predicting

A boosted-tree model learns how Comal Springs and the J17 well responded to temperature and rainfall during the decades before cutbacks existed. Run past 2006, it keeps describing that world — the aquifer as it would have behaved unmanaged. The gap between that line and the gauge readings is what management did.

Step 1TrainSep 1946 – Dec 1989

Weekly minimum spring flow and groundwater level against weekly temperature and precipitation at San Antonio airport, with lagged terms for karst response.

Step 2ValidateJan 1990 – Dec 2005

Still before mandated cutbacks. Prediction and gauge should agree — and they do, at R² ≈ 0.80. This is the control.

Step 3PredictJan 2006 – Dec 2022

Cutbacks now in force. The model does not know that. Where it falls below the gauge, management is the difference.

Step 4Project2023 – 2099

The same two models driven by downscaled CMIP5 and CMIP6 climate, run with and without the cutback logic, to 2100.

The design is what makes the claim testable. If the model were simply bad, it would miss in 1990–2005 too. It does not — it misses only after the rules arrive, and it misses in one direction.

Reading this honestly

What the analysis does and does not establish

It measures the package, not the parts

Critical Period Management is the focus, but forbearance, aquifer storage and recovery, the voluntary irrigation suspension option and regional conservation are all folded into the same signal. The analysis cannot say which one did the work.

Flow is a proxy for habitat

Spring flow and water level stand in for ecosystem health. That is a reasonable assumption for species that need water in the spring runs, but it is an assumption, not a measurement of the salamander, darter or riffle beetle populations themselves.

2011–2015 sits outside the training data

During that drought the Uvalde pool hit its lowest level in two years, cutting east–west transfer into the San Antonio pool. The model never saw conditions like it, which is why the counterfactual occasionally fails to sit below the gauge in those years.

Projections inherit every upstream uncertainty

Emission scenario, climate model, statistical downscaling and the hydrologic model each add error, and a black-swan event absent from 1946–2005 would not be anticipated at all. Treat the spread as the message, not any single line.
